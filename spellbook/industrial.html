Situation

A multi-million-dollar engineering firm that could not say where its own wins came from. It delivers a specialized scope on very large projects: data centres, power generation, oil and gas, industrial sites. Six internal reps, about twenty specialist resellers worldwide, a business run on relationships. They had paid an IT firm to implement Salesforce and nobody used it: no accounts, no contacts, no activity, not even the reps' email. The only thing in it was closed-won and closed-lost, which turned out to be the most useful thing they owned.

The ask was the one every sales background attracts: get us more meetings. Discovery said the problem sat upstream. Nobody could say what share of wins came from which segment or country, and there was no formal view of the revenue streams at all.

The decision

I did not start with outreach. I started by mapping how revenue moves through the business, from the team structure and the win data outward. That produced two segments, new-build and retrofit, each split by industry, and two motions: one triggered when a project is announced, because whoever reaches the right people first tends to get the work, and one always-on.

The map surfaced the thing the whole design hangs on: in this business nothing starts until a project is announced. A project is the spine. Accounts attach to it, contacts to those, outreach and deals hang off the chain. Their sales coordinator, who doubled as the Salesforce admin, saw it as quickly as I did. So I rebuilt Salesforce around a project object rather than bolting a prospecting tool beside it, owned the data layer rather than renting it, and, because I do not know this industry the way their reps do, built the engine to be tuned by them. What I chose not to automate: any conversation, the go/no-go on a sequence, and the ICP itself.

Then the spine turned out to have a gate in front of it. The win data and the reps' own accounts of how deals start showed that the firm usually gets onto a project because it was already on the buyer's pre-approved vendor list, a decision made at the company level, often before any project exists. Nobody tracked it; the reps could not say where they were approved. The recommendation I made in October 2026 is a pre-approval tracker on the account and a second outreach motion aimed at getting onto the list. The project engine finds the work; the pre-approval list decides who is allowed to bid on it.

What I built or changed

  • The revenue map. Segments, sub-segments and motions written down for the first time, and their win history recomputed by segment and country. The firm was focused on net-new data-centre work, and the pilot still is, but the history suggested retrofit was likely their highest-win-rate segment, at smaller deal values. That was news to them, and it changed their annual planning.
  • A project-aligned Salesforce module. New data model with projects at the centre; one company, one record; bidirectional sync so reps never leave Salesforce; an operator dashboard; and ask-the-engine reporting, so anyone can put a question to the engine in plain language and get a report back.
  • The project engine. 13 public sources in the first pilot. Each new project triggers a research pass, then account identification, then people, then a draft written for each person. A generator drafts, a critic challenges, auditors check, and a schema contract rejects anything malformed, because a fluent email to the wrong plant is worse than no email. Underneath it, an owned market layer of 25,000+ companies and 5,500+ contacts across 249 metros, replacing $20–50K a year of paid data.
  • An operator layer the reps tune. The reps correct the engine from inside Salesforce: this title is right, that one is wrong and why; this project belongs in another sub-segment; this company is a fit. Profiles, safeguards and rollback, so a bad correction is one click to undo. The intelligence belongs to the team.
  • Pre-approval tracking. Recommended in October 2026, not yet built. A pre-approved status on the account, with who approved it and when, and outreach to the companies the firm should be approved with before any project exists. Together with the project spine it makes a new thing possible: a project appears, a company attaches, that company has the firm on its list, a rep owns the relationship, and the system tells the rep "now is the time." That alert does not exist yet. It is the next thing the structure makes possible.
  • Priority hierarchy for routing. A human conversation pauses every automated touch below it, and every action has an audit trail that says why it fired or why it waited. Reps trust the machine because it cannot step on them.
  • Handoff discipline. An engine reference, decision logs and a training curriculum, so the client's own team can run it without me.

Stack: TypeScript on Supabase with n8n orchestration and Salesforce as the system of record. Custom code only where Clay and the CRM stopped. Outreach drafted with Anthropic and OpenAI models across 4 angles.

Rules where the answer should never vary. Models where judgment helps and a check can catch a miss. People where a relationship is on the line.

How it ran

The machine waits for people, not the other way round. Ingest runs on a schedule; new projects are researched, scored and drafted, and a draft that fails the critic, an auditor or the schema is dropped, not sent. Before any automated touch, routing checks the hierarchy; if a rep has touched the account, the machine waits. Reps own the record from "qualified meeting booked" and tune the engine as they go. The pilot is one segment in one country, deliberately small, so the signal proves itself before it scales.

Result

The result so far is structural, and I would rather say that plainly than dress it up. The outreach engine is built and the content is being finalized; the first sends are about to start, so there is no meetings or pipeline number yet. When there is one it will be here, with its window and its source.

What has changed is what the business can see. About 95% of new opportunities now land in Salesforce, from an org that held almost nothing, because the reps use it: it is where the projects are, and the record says why each was picked. The firm has a formal map of its revenue for the first time and a reporting layer that answers where wins come from. Because the engine runs along the spine, I could ask it questions about a business I did not know and get answers in revenue terms; several were news to people who had run it for years, and one changed their annual planning. The pre-approval gap, invisible before, is now on the table. A murky business became a list of about thirty things the team knows it can do, and the question became which to do first.

What I'd do differently

I should have found the pre-approval gate sooner. I mapped the spine from projects because that is where the public data is, and only later saw from the win history and the reps that the list position is decided before the project exists. The first design optimized for speed to an announced project; the better design starts a year earlier, at the vendor list, and I would have shaped the account model and the first outreach motion around it from the start. I also built the owned data layer before a single sequence had proven the signal source; next time I would run the first segment on paid data for a month and migrate once public records had shown they predict real conversations. And I wrote seventeen reference documents. Nobody reads seventeen documents. I would ship the decision log and let the rest grow from questions the team actually asks.
