Situation

A lead-generation program built on the side of a desk, and a brief to turn it into a function. Black & White Zebra runs 16 software-buyer publications. The largest, People Managing People, draws about 40K visitors a month. Readers fill in a form asking for software advice, advisors call them, and partner vendors pay for qualified introductions. The former CRO had built the program on the side of his desk and proven it could matter; he described himself as the explorer who had planted the flag, and hired me to build the castle. When I joined in January 2024 the program made about $30K a month. Each step from traffic to revenue was measured somewhere, but not in one place, so nobody could say which step cost the most. I reported to the CEO and COO and owned the program end to end: forecasting, pricing, partner terms, the advisor team and the operating cadence. I was hired off a plan I wrote during the interview process, and it is roughly the plan I followed for the first six months to a year; that is also how I would like to start with you.

The decision

Follow the revenue upstream and fix what the data shows, before asking for anything new. Everything started with traffic, so I mapped every step from it: how much landed on pages with our calls to action, how many clicked, how many finished the form, how many we spoke to, how many we placed, and what each placement was worth, across 16 publications and hundreds of pages each, in a way that rolled up and still drilled into any one line. The first thing it showed was not a traffic problem. Leads were being worked by preference rather than by tier: the two advisors took the most lucrative ones and tier-one and tier-two leads went unworked, with hours of capacity to spare.

What I chose not to do was hire. The obvious ask was more advisors and more reps. I argued for squeezing more out of every lead we already had: territory routing so each advisor owned a region, direct booking into their calendars, a tiering model, and for the first time weekly metrics and coaching with a target of every tier-one-to-three lead attempted. Later, an AI assistant inside the advisory workflow and automated call grading instead of a third advisor, and the compensation case to the CEO and COO to move reps from bonus to margin-based commission, because owned leads were sitting unworked.

The drivers-and-levers model came six to eight months in, and changed how decisions got made. The COO introduced it at a leadership session. It put the whole program on one model from traffic to revenue, reviewed with the executive team on the L10 cadence, and from then on a change got priority only if it would move a line on it. It arrived at the right moment, because the next thing that happened was two shocks at once.

What I built or changed

  • Step-by-step conversion map, then the drivers-and-levers model and L10 dashboard. Every step from traffic to revenue across 16 publications, in one place: the basis for the forecast and, once the model arrived, the agenda for the executive review.
  • Routing, territory and capacity. A three-rep global routing model weighted by volume, revenue and time zone, with direct booking on form submission into the assigned rep's live calendar, in Chili Piper. A territory rollout, a lead-tiering model, and a bottoms-up capacity model (call volume, connect rate, call-length distribution) used to set quota. First response to tier 1–3 leads from about 12 hours to 30 minutes; UK lead utilization 5% to 20%.
  • Bi-weekly experiment program (final six months). A/B tests on the form and funnel, run as a cross-department project with developers, designers and content. I scoped each test, ran kickoff, set dependencies and owned the timeline. Step 1 to step 2 conversion up about 45%, and submissions per CTA click up about 25%, with a knock-on rise in direct bookings.
  • Margin-based compensation. The business case to the CEO and COO to move reps from bonus to commission, then a real-time commission dashboard and rep calculator. Owned-lead utilization 12% to 25%.
  • AI advisor assistant and call grading. An agent with retrieval giving just-in-time guidance inside the live advisory workflow, plus automated call grading. Two advisors covered nine software categories; new-advisor call scores ~30% to 75–80%; lead returns down ~15%.

How it ran

Standardize, then it worked, then the shocks, then the hard choice. The first six months were the fixes above, and they worked: the program went from about $30K a month to about $130K, the buyer base grew about 30%, and because I ran the program I was also pulled into calls where a buyer was thinking of cancelling and into buying calls with the account executives. About 90% of those went our way, with the account manager or the rep doing most of the work, and more buyers meant higher utilization of the leads we already had. The forecast was re-cut as lines moved and stayed inside ±10%.

Two shocks, and the argument I lost

About eight months in, two things hit at once. Generative search began answering the question our pages existed to answer, and I could isolate the drag it put on leads month over month. Separately, someone on another team measured what the exit-intent prompt that fed about 70% of my leads was costing the company in pay-per-click revenue. It was millions a year. They turned it off. That was the right call for the company and a very bad one for my department, and I would have made it too.

The model is what made the next argument possible to have at all. I separated the two shocks in one analysis, a ramp shock and a direct shock, and ran a tornado across the levers to see what was left to pull. Everyone wanted more traffic. I did not control the levers that moved traffic, and generative search was still rising. The analysis said the better bet was to monetize the leads we already had and keep the buyers we still had from leaving, because losing buyers made every remaining lead worth less. I recommended that. I was overruled, and we spent a quarter on content aimed at the program. It ranked, and it barely converted.

The cadence that did work

Of the levers left, website conversion had the most headroom, so that is where the last six months went. Every two weeks: a hypothesis for one step of the funnel, with the baseline a company should be able to hit and the revenue impact if we got there; scoped with the developers for the lift; design and content build the materials; then one test running hands-off while the next was being built. I quarterbacked it, scoped it and kept it on track across developers, design and content. Twenty hypotheses, ranked by effort against reward. Step 1 to step 2 conversion up about 45%, submissions per click up about 25%, direct bookings up with them.

Result

About fourfold in four to six months, from roughly $30K to roughly $130K a month, with the forecast inside ±10% the whole way. The buyer base grew about 30%. The partner program reached $200K+ in under eight months with 20+ HR-tech partners, and HR's share of program revenue rose from about 30% to about 70%. The funnel numbers above are the operating evidence; the revenue number is the one the CEO and COO paid for.

Most of that growth came from two things, and neither was traffic. The first was capacity: once routing, tiering and direct booking were in place and the advisors were measured on working every tier-one-to-three lead, the program was monetizing leads it had been paying to attract and then letting go cold. The second was buyers. More partners, and partners who stayed, meant a higher share of those leads could be placed, which is what the ~30% growth in the buyer base and the saves and closes I sat in on were for. Margin-based compensation kept both of those honest. The funnel test program came later, after the shocks, and its job was to hold the line rather than to drive the climb.

What I'd do differently

Sell the analysis inside the company as hard as I built it. The hardest thing about that period is that I think I had the answers and did not land them. The tornado call was backed by data and I lost it anyway. That is a skill gap I own, not a decision I would undo: next time I bring the model to the executives earlier and let them see the trade-off in their own numbers, rather than arriving with the recommendation. The second thing is structural. I was responsible for the strategy, the build and the execution, largely by myself; the project-management seat was never properly set up, and I covered it. The test cadence showed what one more real pair of hands was worth, and I would have pushed harder for it, or made that role work, a year earlier.
